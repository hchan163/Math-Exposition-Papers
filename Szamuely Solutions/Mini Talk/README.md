## Abstract:

Mathematics is full of connections, but as an average undergraduate makes their foray through foundational areas (e.g. algebra, topology, analysis) few are to be found.
The theory of covering spaces is a rewarding look into what such an intersection looks like - namely, between group theory and topology. This talk will also serve as a crash-course introduction into the world of algebraic topology, which is the study of shapes using algebraic structures (e.g. groups).

Suppose we have some n dimensional object, and we want to be able to better understand its shape more concretely. Of course, this gets exponentially more difficult as n increases, so a natural thing to try and do is to project this object down a few dimensions, and look at its shadow instead. It has a lower dimension so it is naturally easier to understand; and with luck we might even be able to prove things about it. Then the problem is this: Suppose we have a question we want answered about the object, and this question becomes answerable when we look at its shadows. When does knowing the answer about the shadow give us the answer about the original object? More ambitiously, can I figure out what the shape of the object looks like only by collecting enough of its shadows? Unfortunately, in general this is an impossible task. However, as you may surmise, there is a special type of shape and projection under which this problem can be solved for virtually any reasonable question you ask. Such a shape is called a *cover*.

More formally, if X is a topological (metric) space, then a cover of X is a topological (metric) space Y, along with a continuous map f: Y -> X such that each point in X has a open neighbourhood such that its preimage under f decomposes into a disjoint sum of copies of said neighbourhood (I will explain this more in detail during the first talk). It turns out that the information covering spaces can be compressed to a ridiculous degree, and it is in fact possible to determine what Y looks like completely *by seeing what lives above one single point of X* (you will see how in the second talk).

I currently plan on splitting this into two talks - the first one will cover the Galois correspondence for covering spaces, and the second talk will cover the fiber functor equivalence and the monodromy action. As a bonus (and because I am a try hard) I have typed up selected exercises and solutions to the contents of the two talks, which can be found in my github (if you have questions feel free to contact me). Depending on the reception of the lectures I may give further talks on connections to Galois theory, Riemann surfaces and algebraic geometry.

The strict prerequisites for the first talk are metric spaces (continuous functions) and group theory (quotient groups, group actions).
Viewers with a working knowledge of point set topology (homeomorphisms, quotient spaces) and Galois theory (Galois correspondence) will find the talk much more rewarding and easy to digest, but is not strictly necessary.
